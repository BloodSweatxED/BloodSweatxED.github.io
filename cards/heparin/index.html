Heparin & Its Analogues

One family, three tools: unfractionated heparin, low molecular weight heparin, and fondaparinux. They differ in how you monitor them, how the kidney handles them, and how much of an antidote you actually get. Match the drug to the patient in front of you.

How they work

All of them lean on antithrombin

Each agent binds and supercharges antithrombin. The size of the molecule decides which clotting factors get shut off.

  • UFH is big enough to bridge antithrombin to thrombin: blocks both factor IIa and Xa.
  • LMWH is shorter: mostly factor Xa, with some anti-IIa.
  • Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide: factor Xa only.
Follow it

What to monitor

  • UFH: anti-Xa (heparin assay) or aPTT on a weight-based nomogram; ACT for cath-lab / ECMO doses.
  • LMWH: usually none. Check a peak anti-Xa (4 h post-dose) in renal impairment, pregnancy, obesity, or very low weight.
  • Fondaparinux: no routine monitoring.

Many centers now prefer anti-Xa over aPTT for UFH: fewer confounders, faster to therapeutic range.

Clinical Pearl

The antidote shrinks as the molecule shrinks.

Protamine fully reverses UFH, only partially reverses LMWH, and does nothing for fondaparinux. The same gradient runs the other way for half-life and renal clearance.

So when bleeding risk is high, renal function is poor, or a procedure is imminent, the short-acting, fully reversible, renally-independent choice is unfractionated heparin.

The kidney decides

Renal function picks the drug

  • CrCl < 30: LMWH accumulates — reduce dose (e.g. enoxaparin treatment to once daily) or switch to UFH.
  • Fondaparinux is contraindicated at CrCl < 30 and used with caution 30–50.
  • UFH is not renally cleared — the default in severe renal failure and dialysis patients.
Start it (VTE, typical adult)

Common starting doses

UFH · 80 units/kg IV bolus, then 18 units/kg/hr, titrated to nomogram
ENOXAPARIN · 1 mg/kg SC q12h (or 1.5 mg/kg SC q24h)
FONDAPARINUX · 5 / 7.5 / 10 mg SC daily by weight band
  • ACS and periprocedural protocols use different regimens — follow the pathway.
  • Dose-reduce enoxaparin to 1 mg/kg SC q24h when CrCl < 30.

Teaching reference only. Verify every dose against weight, indication, renal function, and your institutional protocol before ordering.

Stop the bleed

Reversal & bleeding

  • UFH: protamine 1 mg per 100 units given in the last ~2–3 h (max ~50 mg/dose, slow IV). Short half-life means stopping the drip often does most of the work.
  • LMWH: protamine partially reverses — 1 mg per 1 mg enoxaparin if within 8 h; expect only ~60% anti-Xa neutralization.
  • Fondaparinux: no effective antidote; protamine does not work. In life-threatening bleeding consider rFVIIa and expert input.
  • Protamine itself can cause hypotension, bradycardia, and anaphylaxis (fish allergy, prior protamine/NPH insulin exposure).
Don't miss it

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

  • Immune, PF4–heparin antibody reaction. Platelets fall > 50%, classically day 5–10 (sooner with exposure in the last ~100 days).
  • The danger is thrombosis, not bleeding. Use the 4Ts score to gauge pretest probability.
  • Stop all heparin — including LMWH and line flushes. Start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or a DOAC).
  • Do not transfuse platelets for prophylaxis and do not start warfarin until platelets recover.
  • Risk order: UFH > LMWH > fondaparinux (lowest).
Before you order

Hold / rethink

  • Active major bleeding or very high bleeding risk
  • Prior HIT — avoid all heparins, including LMWH
  • Severe thrombocytopenia or known coagulopathy
  • Neuraxial anesthesia timing (respect LMWH intervals)
  • Poor renal function narrowing your options
