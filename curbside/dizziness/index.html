The Curbside · Stop 08

Dizzy is the beginning.

Dizziness is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Start with the patient’s words. Let timing, triggers, and a targeted examination guide the next move.

The curbside question: What pattern is this, what could hurt this patient, and which examination actually fits?

Ask. Then anchor.

The adjective is not the diagnosis

Start open: “What do you mean by dizzy?” Let the patient describe it. Then establish onset, duration, triggers, associated symptoms, and baseline function. Symptom labels overlap and can change during the interview.

01 · Timing

What happened first?

Exact onset and last known well. Continuous symptoms or discrete spells? How long does each spell last? Fully back to baseline between episodes?

02 · Triggers

What brings it on?

Rolling in bed? Standing up? Exertion? Does the movement start a new spell from baseline, or worsen dizziness already present?

03 · Company

What came with it?

Hearing change, tinnitus, headache, neck pain, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, weakness, numbness, chest pain, palpitations, bleeding, or loss of consciousness?

04 · Function

What can they do?

Vitals, targeted neurologic and cardiac examination, eye movements, coordination, and gait when safe. Record inability to sit, stand, or walk. Do not force an unsafe walking trial.

Red line: sudden neurologic symptoms.

New focal deficits, severe truncal or gait ataxia, new unilateral hearing loss with continuous vertigo, or concerning new head/neck pain require urgent evaluation for a central cause. Vascular risks raise concern; their absence does not exclude stroke. Hypertension alone does not localize the lesion.

Timing → syndrome → test.

Movement makes many things worse
AVS

Continuous

Acute vestibular syndrome: acute, persistent dizziness with nausea, motion intolerance, and imbalance, often with nystagmus. Symptoms typically persist for days.

Consider vestibular neuritis and posterior circulation stroke. Do not wait 24 hours to evaluate a newly continuous syndrome.

Next: targeted neurologic, gait, and eligible oculomotor examination.

s-EVS

Spontaneous spells

Discrete episodes without an obligate trigger. Consider vestibular migraine, Ménière disease, TIA, and cardiac or systemic causes.

A normal examination between spells does not exclude TIA. If TIA is suspected, pursue a vascular evaluation.

t-EVS

Triggered spells

Brief spells reproducibly started by a specific movement, with recovery toward baseline between spells.

Head position: consider BPPV. Standing: consider orthostatic symptoms.

Use Dix–Hallpike for suspected posterior canal BPPV. A typical positive test supports Epley treatment. Atypical findings need reassessment.

Look for a true trigger.

“Turning my head makes it worse” does not establish BPPV. Continuous dizziness worsened by movement remains continuous dizziness.

Source: SAEM GRACE-3

HINTS+ has an entry requirement.

A trained examination, not a checkbox

Use only in the right patient.

HINTS is for a currently symptomatic patient with acute vestibular syndrome and nystagmus, examined by a clinician trained in its use. It is not a screening test for every dizzy patient, resolved spells, presyncope, or typical BPPV. Without nystagmus, emphasize gait severity and the broader neurologic examination.

Dangerous findings

Central concern

  • Normal head impulse in an eligible AVS patient.
  • Gaze-evoked nystagmus that changes direction with gaze.
  • Vertical ocular realignment on alternate cover testing: skew.
  • New unilateral hearing loss adds concern for stroke.
Next step

Act on uncertainty

A central or equivocal examination needs stroke evaluation and MRI/MRA. If a trained examiner is unavailable, use the local stroke/imaging pathway; do not document a reassuring HINTS you cannot reliably perform.

An abnormal head impulse alone does not exclude stroke. Early diffusion MRI can miss posterior circulation infarction. Persistent clinical concern requires reassessment despite negative imaging.

Imaging trap: a normal noncontrast head CT does not rule out posterior circulation ischemia. Imaging should answer the clinical question; CT may still be appropriate for hemorrhage or other indications.

Source: Kattah et al., HINTS study · GRACE-3

Translate the sensation.

Useful vocabulary. Incomplete sorting.
“The room moves.”

Vertigo

An illusion of motion: spinning, tilting, rocking, or moving. It can arise from peripheral or central vestibular disease.

BPPV, vestibular neuritis, and Ménière disease are peripheral disorders. Vestibular migraine is a migraine disorder; do not label it an inner-ear lesion.

“I might pass out.”

Presyncope

A sense of impending loss of consciousness. Ask about posture, exertion, prodrome, palpitations, medications, fluid loss, and bleeding.

Consider volume depletion, orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia, structural cardiac disease, and vasovagal physiology. Obtain an ECG; assess orthostatic vitals when safe and indicated. A positive orthostatic result does not close the differential.

“I cannot keep my balance.”

Disequilibrium

Imbalance while upright or walking. Compare with baseline. Acute onset needs a central cause considered.

Chronic contributors include neuropathy, parkinsonism, cerebellar disease, impaired vision, and medications. Multiple causes can coexist.

“I just feel off.”

Other or mixed

Review medications, substances, glucose when indicated, and clues to anemia or metabolic illness. Order tests to answer specific concerns.

Anxiety and hyperventilation can cause lightheadedness and tingling. Do not diagnose anxiety from age or a normal routine neurologic examination alone. Evaluate plausible dangerous causes and look for a compatible pattern.

NIDCD: balance disorders · Vestibular migraine consensus · AHA: syncope

When to consider seizure.

A specific story earns a specific test

Brief, stereotyped spells with altered awareness, automatisms, déjà vu, or post-event confusion can suggest focal seizures. Ask a witness what happened. Isolated unexplained dizziness is not, by itself, an indication for screening EEG.

If the history suggests a seizure, arrange appropriate neurologic assessment and consider EEG to support diagnosis. A normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy.

Source: NICE NG217

The bedside takeaway.

One symptom. Several systems.
DESCRIBE → TIME → TRIGGER → EXAMINE → TARGET TESTING

For patients: You do not need the perfect word. Describe when it started, how long it lasts, what brings it on, and what else happens.

Get emergency care.

Sudden dizziness with new weakness, speech or vision changes, inability to stand or walk, severe new headache or neck pain, chest pain, or collapse warrants emergency care. Call emergency services and do not drive yourself.
