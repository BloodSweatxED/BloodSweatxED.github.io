Bedside TTE:
The Five Views.

POCUS echo isn't a formal echocardiogram — it's five views answering five questions: How's the squeeze? Is there fluid around the heart? Is the RV blown out? How full is the tank? Is a valve grossly wrong?

Every view below is shown the way you'll actually learn it: where the probe sits, then the image that probe position produces. Every advanced cardiac application — including Echo for PE (No. 003) — is built on these windows.

When to scan

The undifferentiated patient's exam

Reach for the probe

  • Hypotension or shock — pump, tank, or obstruction?
  • Dyspnea: failing LV vs. everything else
  • Chest pain with an abnormal ECG: wall motion, effusion, dissection clues
  • Cardiac arrest: organized activity vs. standstill, and the reversible causes
  • Pre-intubation hemodynamic screen in the crashing patient

Stay in your lane

  • POCUS echo is qualitative and focused. Formal echo quantifies — regional wall motion scoring, valve gradients, diastology.
  • "Grossly normal" is a legitimate POCUS answer; "normal echocardiogram" is not.
  • One bad view is not an exam. If a window fails, move to the next — the five views back each other up.

Setup

Cardiac preset changes the rules

Probe

Phased array

Small footprint fits between ribs. Cardiac preset — and note the screen indicator flips to the right side in cardiac mode.

Ergonomics

Machine on the right

Stand on the patient's right when possible: right hand drives the probe, left hand works the machine. Hold the probe like a pencil, hand anchored on the chest.

Position

Supine → LLD

Left lateral decubitus swings the heart against the chest wall and clears lung out of the way — it rescues difficult parasternal and apical windows.

Parasternal long axis (PLAX)

3rd–5th ICS, left sternal border · indicator to the right shoulder

Your orientation view and the one that fails least often. Everything is laid out in a single plane, so it doubles as a quality check: if the aortic and mitral valves aren't both open in the same frame, you're off axis.

Best view for: gestalt LV function (do the walls thicken, and does the mitral anterior leaflet nearly touch the septum?), pericardial effusion, gross aortic root pathology.
Don't miss: the descending aorta behind the LA. Fluid anterior to it is pericardial; fluid tracking posterior to it is pleural. That one landmark decides which consult you call.

Parasternal short axis (PSAX)

From PLAX, rotate ~90° clockwise · indicator to the left shoulder · papillary muscle level

The LV in cross-section, squeezing concentrically. Sweep the whole ventricle rather than parking at one level: the mitral "fish mouth" at the base, papillary muscles in the middle, the apex beyond.

Best view for: global squeeze at a glance, septal flattening / D-sign (RV pressure or volume overload), and gross regional wall motion by territory.
Don't miss: the septum's shape through the cardiac cycle. A round LV that goes D-shaped is doing something a number can't tell you.

Apical 4-chamber (A4C)

PMI / inframammary fold · aim toward the right shoulder · indicator to the patient's left

This is the view for chamber comparison. The RV should be roughly 60% the size of the LV, and the apex should belong to the LV. When the RV starts sharing the apex, that's your abnormal.

Best view for: RV:LV ratio, TAPSE, McConnell's sign, and valve gestalt with color across all four chambers at once.
Don't miss: foreshortening. One space too high slices the ventricle obliquely — fake small LV, fake big RV, fake wall motion. Find the true apex before you trust the ratio.

Subxiphoid 4-chamber

Probe flat under the xiphoid, aimed up to the left shoulder · liver as the window

The view that works when the chest doesn't: COPD, mechanical ventilation, chest dressings, active CPR. It costs nothing to keep it in the rotation and it will save you on the patient whose parasternal window doesn't exist.

Best view for: effusion and tamponade physiology (RV diastolic collapse), cardiac activity in arrest, and imaging around compressions.
Don't miss: the clock. In arrest, probe on the skin before compressions pause, record ten seconds, resume, then review the clip while CPR continues.

IVC

From subxiphoid, rotate sagittal · trace into the RA · measure ~2 cm from the junction

Size plus respiratory collapse gives a rough read on right atrial pressure: small and collapsing suggests an empty tank, fat and fixed suggests a full or obstructed one. It's a trend tool, not a number to worship.

  • ≤2.1 cm with >50% collapse → low right atrial pressure
  • >2.1 cm with <50% collapse → high right atrial pressure
  • Positive-pressure ventilation inverts the respiratory changes — interpret accordingly
Don't miss: the impostor. Confirm your vessel drains into the RA and takes a hepatic vein. The aorta is round, thick-walled, pulsatile, and to the patient's left — and it has fooled everyone at least once.

What the pathology looks like

Pattern recognition, not measurement

Normal is a shape you learn by repetition. These are the six abnormals that change what you do in the next ten minutes.

PLAX · Subxiphoid

Pericardial effusion

Anechoic stripe tracking around the heart, inside the bright pericardium. In PLAX it runs anterior to the descending aorta — that's what separates it from a pleural effusion.

Subxiphoid · A4C

Tamponade physiology

Effusion plus RV free-wall collapse in diastole plus a plethoric IVC. Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis that echo supports — the patient, not the picture, decides.

PSAX

D-sign

The septum flattens and the round LV goes D-shaped as the RV overwhelms it. Systolic flattening points to pressure overload, diastolic to volume — full workup in No. 003.

A4C

RV strain

RV approaching or exceeding LV size and starting to claim the apex. Add TAPSE (<17 mm abnormal) and look for McConnell's: free wall akinesis with a preserved apex.

PLAX · PSAX

Poor squeeze

Walls barely thicken, the cavity stays big through systole, and the mitral anterior leaflet never comes near the septum. Wide EPSS is the training-wheels version of a low EF.

IVC

Flat vs. plethoric IVC

The two ends of the tank. Flat and collapsing in a hypotensive patient argues for volume; fat and fixed argues against it and pushes you toward pump or obstruction.

Pearls & pitfalls

Where this scan goes wrong
Pearl

Rib space, not force

A bad parasternal window is usually one rib space too high or too low, not insufficient pressure. Slide, don't press. Then try LLD.

Pitfall

Pericardial vs. pleural fluid

In PLAX, pericardial effusion tracks anterior to the descending aorta; pleural effusion runs posterior to it. One gets a pericardiocentesis consult, the other a pigtail conversation.

Pitfall

Epicardial fat pad panic

An isolated anterior hypoechoic stripe that moves with the heart and contains speckles is usually fat, not effusion. True effusions are usually circumferential or dependent first.

Pearl

EPSS is your training-wheels EF

In PLAX, if the mitral valve's anterior leaflet nearly slaps the septum each beat (E-point septal separation <7 mm), the EF is probably fine. Big gap, bad pump — caveats for MS and AI.

Pitfall

Foreshortening the apex

An A4C obtained one space too high slices the ventricle obliquely: fake small LV, fake big RV, fake wall motion. Find the true apex before trusting anything the view tells you.

Pearl

In arrest, subxiphoid owns the pulse check

Ten seconds, probe already in place before compressions pause, record the loop, resume. Review the clip while CPR continues — don't let the ultrasound stretch the pause.
