Knee
Arthrocentesis.

The hot, swollen knee has one question that cannot wait: is it septic? No exam finding, inflammatory marker, or gestalt answers it. The fluid answers it — which makes the tap the workup, not an optional extra.

The knee is also the most forgiving joint to tap: a large target, a superficial pouch, and an approach that keeps the needle away from everything important.

When to tap

And what stops you

Tap it

  • Atraumatic hot swollen joint — septic arthritis versus crystal disease is a fluid diagnosis
  • Monoarthritis in a febrile patient, an immunocompromised patient, or anyone with bacteremia risk
  • Suspected crystal arthropathy needing confirmation before committing to the label
  • Tense, painful effusion — therapeutic drainage is legitimate on its own

Adjust or hold

  • Cellulitis or broken skin over every approach — don't drag infection into a sterile joint; find clean skin or image-guide another window
  • Prosthetic joint: call orthopedics first — many departments want these tapped under their protocol
  • Significant coagulopathy is a relative issue — a septic joint still needs fluid; check your institutional guidance
  • Fracture into the joint changes the conversation — image first when trauma is the story

Setup

Big needle, relaxed quad

Kit

18–20 g

Large-bore needle, 20–60 mL syringe (with a hemostat to swap syringes on big effusions), sterile prep, tubes: cell count, culture, crystals.

Position

Supine, knee ~20°

Slight flexion over a towel roll relaxes the quadriceps and opens the retropatellar space. A tense quad clamps the patella down and shrinks your target.

Prep

Mark then clean

Mark the entry point before prepping. Local anesthetic to skin and track per institutional reference. Ultrasound confirms and localizes small effusions.

The technique

4 steps · superolateral approach

Find the corner

1 cm above and 1 cm lateral to the superolateral patellar border

Palpate the superolateral corner of the patella with the knee slightly flexed and the quad soft. The entry point sits one fingerbreadth up and one out from it (Fig. 1). Ballotte the patella first — a floating patella confirms an effusion worth the needle.

Advance under the patella

45° under the patella · aspirate continuously · fluid usually within 1–3 cm

Direct the needle beneath the patella toward the center of the joint, pulling gentle vacuum the whole way. Fluid return is your endpoint. If you hit bone, you're either on femur (drop the angle, aim more superficial, stay under the patella) or on patella (steepen slightly).

Drain it dry

Milk the joint toward the needle · swap syringes with a hemostat

Compress the medial joint and suprapatellar pouch with your free hand to push fluid toward the needle. When the syringe fills, steady the hub with a hemostat and exchange syringes rather than re-puncturing. Complete drainage is both your diagnostic sample and most of the patient's pain relief.

Dry tap with an obvious effusion? The needle is usually in the fat pad or pre-patellar bursa. Ultrasound the pouch, confirm depth, and redirect — don't conclude "no effusion" from a missed pocket.

Send the right studies

Cell count + differential · gram stain + culture · crystal analysis

Three studies answer the question; everything else is decoration. Note gross appearance in the chart — clear yellow, cloudy, purulent, or bloody — because the lab numbers will lag your disposition decision. Bandage, and no prolonged immobilization.

Reading the fluid

The numbers that matter
FluidWBC (per µL)Means
Normal< 200Clear, viscous, essentially acellular
Non-inflammatory200–2,000Osteoarthritis, mechanical — clear to straw-colored
Inflammatory2,000–50,000Gout, pseudogout, rheumatoid — cloudy, low viscosity. Crystals make the diagnosis.
Septic (typical)> 50,000Purulent; PMN-predominant (>90%). But no cutoff excludes infection — treat the patient, not the threshold
CrystalsseenNeedle-shaped, negatively birefringent = gout; rhomboid, positive = pseudogout. Crystals and infection can coexist.
HemarthrosisbloodyTrauma (think ligament or occult fracture), anticoagulation, or bleeding diathesis

The overlap zones are where patients get hurt: a septic joint can present under 50,000 WBC, especially partially treated, immunocompromised, or prosthetic. Gram stain misses half of septic arthritis. If the story says septic, the fluid's borderline numbers don't acquit it.

Pearls & pitfalls

Where this goes wrong
Pearl

The quad is the gatekeeper

Most "difficult" knee taps are a tense quadriceps pressing the patella into the femur. A towel roll under the knee, a relaxed patient, and a moment of coaching open the space better than a bigger needle.

Pearl

Crystals don't clear the joint

A patient with known gout gets septic arthritis at higher rates, not lower. Seeing urate crystals is not permission to stop thinking — the culture still has to come back.

Pitfall

Talking yourself out of the tap

"Improved with NSAIDs," "no fever," "normal CRP" — none of these reliably excludes septic arthritis. The joint that made you consider tapping is the joint that needs tapping.

Pitfall

Through the cellulitis

Passing a needle through infected skin can seed a sterile joint. Choose an approach through clean skin — medial parapatellar if the lateral side is angry — or use ultrasound to find a clean window.

Pitfall

The under-filled crystal tube

A few drops sent for "all studies" produces a cell count and nothing else. Prioritize: count and culture first, crystals next, and say so on the requisition when volume is short.

Pearl

Drainage is therapy

A tense effusion is a large part of the pain. Draining the knee dry treats the patient while it diagnoses them — and a re-accumulating effusion within hours is itself information.
